§ 01 — The problem

"All customers" is not a strategy.

A convenience chain that treats every shopper the same wastes its marketing budget. But the raw data — baskets, category spends, line items — doesn't hand you groups; it hands you thousands of rows of behaviour. The job was to find the natural seams in that behaviour and describe them in language a store manager would recognise.

The target was interpretability first: five segments, each with a plain "pen portrait" and a clear marketing recommendation — not a cluster diagram nobody can act on.

Retail spend is skewed. Model it as if it isn't and you cluster the outliers.

§ 02 — Constraints

Rules for trustworthy segments.

  • Segments must be interpretable.

    A cluster nobody can name is useless to a marketer. Every group needed a behavioural story, not just a centroid.

  • Handle skew honestly.

    Retail spend and quantities are heavily skewed with fat tails. Winsorisation and log transforms stop a handful of big spenders from defining every cluster.

  • Let the data pick the K.

    Five clusters were chosen against inertia and silhouette scores, not decided in advance for tidiness.

§ 03 — Architecture

From four tables to five groups.

Four datasets — customer summaries, baskets, category spends, and line items — were joined into customer-level features, cleaned of skew, reduced with PCA, and clustered with K-Means.

PCA

04 — Cluster
K-Means
Five clusters chosen by inertia + silhouette, then profiled into pen portraits.

Category spend became proportional preference features rather than raw pounds — so a light spender who buys mostly tobacco lands next to a heavy one with the same tilt, which is the behaviour marketing actually cares about.

§ 04 — What the clusters revealed

One segment carries the value.

The five groups split cleanly: Core Grocery Shoppers with large, diverse baskets; Tobacco Convenience Buyers and Drinks & Tobacco Shoppers around focused convenience purchases; Cashpoint Users making small trips tied to cash withdrawals; and a very small Lottery-Focused group.

The recommendation followed the money: prioritise Core Grocery Shoppers for loyalty rewards and basket-building, use point-of-sale promotions for the tobacco and drinks segments, place impulse offers near the cashpoint, and run lottery-linked nudges for the last group.

§ 05 — Turning clusters into decisions

A recommendation per segment.

Statistics don't move a marketing budget — recommendations do. Each cluster got a short statistical summary and a concrete action, so the deliverable read as a plan rather than a plot. That translation step is the part that makes segmentation worth doing.

§ 06 — What I'd do differently

Three things, next time.

  • Test soft clustering.

    Some shoppers genuinely straddle segments; a Gaussian mixture would capture that overlap instead of forcing a hard assignment.

  • Add stability testing.

    Re-running K-Means on bootstrapped samples would show how durable each segment really is before a team bets a campaign on it.

  • Tie segments to a value estimate.

    Attaching a rough lifetime-value figure to each group would rank the marketing recommendations by expected return, not just intuition.
