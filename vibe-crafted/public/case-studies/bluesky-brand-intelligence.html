§ 01 — The problem

"Who has better engagement" is the shallow question.

Comparing two media brands by follower count or like totals tells you almost nothing about how they occupy a platform. Wired and The Verge cover overlapping ground, but the shape of the conversation around each — its themes, its tone, its network of voices — is where the real difference lives.

The goal was a brand-intelligence read: not a scoreboard, but a multi-angle comparison of sentiment, topics, network structure, and positioning, each stage building on cleaned, shared data.

Engagement counts the noise. Topics and networks find the signal.

§ 02 — Constraints

Discipline for a multi-stage pipeline.

  • One stage per notebook.

    Collection, cleaning, analysis, sentiment, topics, network, and perceptual map each live in their own notebook, so any stage can be rerun without re-collecting data.

  • Raw and processed kept separate.

    Raw captures are frozen; processed datasets and JSON summaries are derived — reproducible from the raw layer, never edited in place.

  • Every figure is an artefact.

    Each chart and table writes to outputs/ with a stable index, so the report cites files rather than screenshots.

§ 03 — Architecture

Seven stages, cleanly chained.

The pipeline runs in order — collect, clean, analyse, score sentiment, model topics, build networks, map positioning — each reading the previous stage's processed output.

Bluesky pull

03 — Analyse

04 — Position
Network + map
Keyword networks, micro-influencer scores, and a brand perceptual map.

The most interesting stage is the network work: keyword co-occurrence graphs plus centrality scoring surface the micro-influencers — smaller accounts that sit structurally central to a brand's conversation and would be cheap, high-leverage partners.

§ 04 — What the pipeline surfaced

Different themes, different tones, different maps.

Stacking the stages produced a genuinely multi-dimensional comparison: engagement distributions and posting-time heatmaps, sentiment splits over a rolling window, distinct LDA topic clusters per brand, and finally a perceptual map placing the two brands against each other on derived axes.

The payoff of the layered approach is that no single chart is asked to carry the verdict — the perceptual map is only trustworthy because the sentiment and topic stages beneath it are visible and reproducible.

§ 05 — From analysis to recommendation

Scores that point at people.

The deliverable ends with recommendations, not just descriptions: per-brand micro-influencer shortlists ranked by network centrality, and a positioning read from the perceptual map. That's the shift from social analytics to social intelligence — output a marketing team could act on tomorrow.

§ 06 — What I'd do differently

Three things, next time.

  • Use a transformer sentiment model.

    Lexicon sentiment struggles with sarcasm and tech jargon; a fine-tuned transformer would read tone on this domain more reliably.

  • Track topics over time.

    Dynamic topic modelling would show how each brand's themes shift week to week, not just their static mix.

  • Orchestrate the notebooks.

    Seven notebooks run by hand invites drift; a small pipeline runner would guarantee the stages execute in the right order every time.
