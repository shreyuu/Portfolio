§ 01 — The problem

Accuracy alone can't carry a decision.

A business risk model that outputs "high risk" with no calibrated probability and no reasoning is a black box a manager can't defend. In a business-analytics setting the model's legibility — can you trust its confidence, can you explain its call — matters as much as its raw accuracy.

So this was built as a complete pipeline whose deliverable isn't just a score: it's a calibrated probability and a decision-tree diagram that shows the path to each prediction.

An uncalibrated classifier is confident in exactly the wrong way.

§ 02 — Constraints

What the workflow had to deliver.

  • Reproducible end to end.

    Preprocessing and modelling saved together as a single pipeline, so the exact transforms travel with the model into .joblib and .pkl artefacts.

  • Honest probabilities.

    A calibrated version of the final pipeline, so predicted probabilities can be read as real likelihoods rather than arbitrary model scores.

  • Explainable to a non-specialist.

    A decision-tree visualisation that a stakeholder can follow without a stats background.

§ 03 — Architecture

Six stages, saved as artefacts.

The notebook moves from data to a shipped model: load and clean, explore, engineer features, train and compare, calibrate the winner, and export the whole pipeline.

Handle missing values, format variables, understand feature meaning.

Calibrate + export
Calibrate probabilities, save the pipeline, render the decision tree.

Saving the whole pipeline — not just the estimator — means the preprocessing can never drift out of sync with the model at inference time. It is the difference between a model that works in the notebook and one that works when someone loads the .pkl months later.

§ 04 — What earned its keep

Calibration changed the usable output.

The model comparison picked a strong classifier on the metrics summary, but the step that made the output usable was calibration. Raw classifier scores clustered in ways that misrepresented real risk; after calibration, a 0.7 could be read as roughly a 70% chance — which is the only form a probability can safely inform a business action.

The decision-tree visualisation then did the explaining, turning the final model from a verdict into a traceable argument.

§ 05 — Explaining the model

A diagram beats a coefficient table.

The decision-tree render was chosen deliberately as the explanation artefact: a stakeholder can trace a single prediction down the branches and see exactly which thresholds drove it. That legibility is why a slightly simpler, explainable model can be the right business choice over a marginally more accurate opaque one.

§ 06 — What I'd do differently

Three things, next time.

  • Add SHAP alongside the tree.

    Per-prediction SHAP values would explain the ensemble models too, not only the single decision tree.

  • Show a calibration curve.

    A reliability plot before and after calibration would make the probability improvement visible instead of asserted.

  • Version the artefacts cleanly.

    Multiple .joblib and .pkl files accumulated; a single named, versioned artefact would remove ambiguity about which model is final.
