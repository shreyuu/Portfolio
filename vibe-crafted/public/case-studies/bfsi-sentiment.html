§ 01 — The problem

Financial sentiment is its own dialect.

"Bank misses estimates" is negative; "regulator eases capital rules" is positive — and a general-purpose sentiment model, trained on movie reviews and tweets, has no idea. BFSI language is terse, domain-loaded, and full of words that mean the opposite of their everyday sense.

The task was to classify sector headlines into positive, negative, and neutral, and — just as importantly — to find out which family of model earns its cost here. Is a fine-tuned transformer worth the training time over a well-tuned gradient-boosted classifier?

The best model is the one whose cost you can justify.

§ 02 — Constraints

Keeping the comparison fair.

  • One pipeline, many models.

    Every model sees the same cleaned, split data, so accuracy differences reflect the model — not a lucky preprocessing choice.

  • Domain-specific text cleaning.

    Custom financial stopwords and careful tokenisation, stemming, and lemmatisation, because generic stopword lists throw away signal in this domain.

  • Validate with stratified folds.

    Stratified K-fold keeps the class balance stable across folds so the headline accuracy figure isn't an artefact of one split.

§ 03 — Architecture

Clean, vectorise, model, ensemble.

The workflow runs in a single notebook: exploratory analysis and word clouds, then a text pipeline, then a ladder of models from logistic regression up to a fine-tuned BERT, with voting and stacking on top.

04 — Combine
Ensemble
Voting + stacking, with confidence scoring and multi-model consensus.

Prediction works two ways: a single headline returns a label and a confidence, and a batch returns a consensus across the top models — a small hedge against any one classifier's blind spot.

§ 04 — What the numbers said

BERT wins on accuracy, ensembles win on value.

The headline result: ensemble methods reached roughly 94–96% accuracy, and a fine-tuned BERT nudged slightly higher — at the cost of by far the longest training time. XGBoost and LightGBM landed in the low-90s while training in a fraction of the time.

For most of this dataset, the gradient-boosted ensemble was the sweet spot: nearly BERT-level accuracy, a small fraction of the compute, and far easier to reason about. BERT earned its keep only on the genuinely ambiguous headlines.

§ 05 — What earned its keep

Preprocessing beat model swaps.

The single biggest accuracy jump didn't come from a fancier model — it came from the domain-specific cleaning. Financial stopwords and careful n-gram features lifted every model at once, which is a cheaper and more durable win than chasing the next architecture.

Feature-importance analysis then paid that back in interpretability: you could point at the exact financial terms driving a prediction, which matters more in a regulated domain than a half-point of accuracy.

§ 06 — What I'd do differently

Three things, next time.

  • Fine-tune BERT on a larger domain corpus.

    The transformer's edge would widen with more BFSI-specific training text; the current gap over ensembles is narrow partly for lack of data.

  • Report per-class F1, not just accuracy.

    Neutral is the easy class and inflates the headline number; per-class precision and recall tell the honest story.

  • Ship a real prediction interface.

    The consensus predictor is the useful artefact — it belongs behind a small web app, not buried in a notebook cell.
